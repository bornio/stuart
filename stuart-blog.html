Meet Stuart: The Agent That Interviews You, Then Builds the Agent That Replaces Your Busywork

Most AI automations fail before the first run because we automate steps instead of outcomes—and forget the unspoken judgment calls that make the job actually work. Stuart is a zero-code Claude plugin designed to fix that.

Repository: bornio/stuart · Inspired by: dr eggbot by @poteto · Read time: ~8 min

Ask someone what job they want to hand off to an AI agent, and they will almost always give you the wrong answer. They’ll say “answer my DMs,” or “send the follow-up email,” or “check this spreadsheet every morning.” They describe a motion, not a mission. And even when they do explain the mission, ninety percent of how they actually pull it off lives in muscle memory—which inbox wins when two records disagree, what “done” really means, and which edge cases make them pause and pick up the phone.

Stuart is a plugin for the Claude desktop app (and Claude Code CLI) built around a refreshingly stubborn premise: don’t let an AI build an agent until it has actually understood the job. Rather than acting as a do-everything assistant, Stuart is a meta-agent. Its single purpose is to sit down with you, unpack a task you do by hand, capture your real-world judgment calls, wire up feedback and safety checks, and then generate a tightly scoped Claude agent—plus its schedules—to do the work without you.

What makes the repository genuinely interesting to inspect is how little machinery it takes to do this well. There is no server, no database, no build step, and not a single line of Python or TypeScript. The entire project is 1 agent file, 8 Markdown skills, 1 scheduling guide, and 2 tiny plugin manifests. It is pure operations design encoded as prompts—and it works just as smoothly for a small-business owner who has never opened a terminal as it does for a software engineer managing a team repository.

The Four-Stage Conversation That Prevents Bad Automations

When you tell Claude “I want to stop doing X” (or invoke /stuart:automate-anything), Stuart does not rush to generate a prompt. Instead, it runs a strict four-stage dialogue. Each stage ends with something concrete you have to confirm, and Stuart saves the running plan after each stage so you can close your laptop and pick up later.

1

Find the Goal — and Kill Bad Ideas Early

stuart:find-the-goal

Stuart’s first rule is blunt: a step is not a goal. If you say you want an agent to send follow-up emails, Stuart asks what goes wrong when they don’t get sent, who notices, and what they lose—repeating until you land on a measurable outcome someone would actually pay for or miss (for example: “Every booking request gets a confirmed slot within 4 hours during business hours, without you touching it”).

Right after agreeing on the goal and its job shape, Stuart runs a quick feasibility check before wasting a minute on process mapping:

  • Connector reality check: Does Claude actually have connectors for the apps involved? If the job relies on login-walled or anti-bot sites like LinkedIn, Instagram, or SSO/2FA portals, Stuart warns you plainly instead of promising fragile browser clicking.
  • Don’t rebuild native features: If software you already pay for does the job natively—ad-platform rules, budget alerts, Google Alerts—Stuart tells you to use that first.
  • Deadline vs. trigger math: Email and chat polling cannot satisfy sub-hourly SLAs. Stuart catches impossible timing upfront.

2

Capture the Process — Through Real Failures

stuart:capture-the-process

You do most of your repetitive work on autopilot, which makes writing instructions from scratch painfully incomplete. Stuart extracts that tacit knowledge in four passes:

  • Messy brain dump: Talk it out (even by voice) without organizing—or point Stuart at 2–3 past threads, docs, or sent emails to read without acting so it can draft the workflow for you to correct.
  • Play back with marked guesses: Stuart condenses your dump into 5–8 plain steps, explicitly flagging every assumption it had to guess so you can fix them.
  • Three real cases (one that went wrong): Stuart walks three concrete examples through your steps out loud. That surfaces the real rules: Which source wins when an email says 3pm and the booking app says 4pm? What does “done” mean? How do we track open work so a retry doesn’t double-charge someone? When should the agent stop and hand back?
  • Prune for a better way: Because the goal outranks the process, Stuart checks every step against Stage 1 and offers to drop dead weight or replace manual steps with a connector or tiny script—while still respecting your preference if you want to keep your way.

Domain-specific safety rules come baked in automatically: external drafts must cite sources for every number and flag unsourced claims instead of guessing; public outreach saves two scrubbed full-text examples you loved, one you’d never send, and a list of banned words; and for people or money data (candidates, payroll, contracts), agent memory stores IDs and links only—never raw contents, never ranking or rejecting people.

3

Close the Loop — Visibility, Feedback, and Triggers

stuart:close-the-loop

An agent that drops reports into an unread tab is dead on arrival. Stage 3 wires up four operational pieces:

  • Delivery where you already look: Drafts go into Gmail, urgent alerts go to a Slack DM to yourself, and open cases live in the Notion table, Google Sheet, or CRM you already open—never buried only in Claude’s Scheduled tab.
  • Readable feedback sources: Stuart asks, “If you did this yourself, where would you find out it went badly?” Your edits to its drafts, customer replies, Google/Yelp reviews, and refunds all become sources the agent can read. When something misfires, it matches the failure by a shared case ID, replays its saved test cases plus the new failure on paper, reports every mismatch (never hiding a conflict when your policy may have evolved), and proposes an edit to the agent file for your approval.
  • The right trigger & home: Local desktop tasks for work needing local files or plugins; cloud tasks for jobs that must run with your laptop closed (including GitHub event triggers and HTTP webhooks).
  • An independent goal check: A second, completely separate scheduled task that verifies the outcome itself—explained in detail below.
4

Build, Dry-Run, and Graduate Safely

stuart:design-an-agent

Only now does Stuart touch disk. It first checks your existing ~/.claude/agents/ and .claude/agents/ folders—extending an existing agent if one already owns the job, or copying working conventions from a sibling. Then it writes a crisp Markdown agent file, reads it back to verify the YAML frontmatter and tool names, and creates any scheduled tasks paused.

Before anything runs unattended, Stuart hands you a ready-to-paste dry-run prompt for a fresh Code tab session. The new agent walks through your saved real cases using only the provided facts—without sending, paying, or mutating anything live—and tells you everywhere its decision differs from the right outcome.

The Permission Line: What Gets “Always Allow” (and What Never Does)

Once the dry run passes, you run the scheduled task manually once via Run now to answer permission prompts. Stuart draws a bright, non-negotiable line between safe autonomy and irreversible side effects:

✓ Safe for “Always Allow”

  • Reading inboxes, trackers, calendars, and docs
  • Saving drafts (Gmail drafts, doc drafts)
  • Updating your own tracking Sheet or Notion table
  • Notifying yourself (Slack DM or email to self)

✕ Never “Always Allow”

  • Sending emails or messages to other people
  • Posting publicly, publishing, or merging code
  • Paying money or changing CRM / database records
  • Creating accounts, granting access, or deleting

Even when you eventually decide to hand over that final send or publish step, Stuart refuses to treat “undo” as a safety net. Graduating an agent past drafting requires a per-item verification check paired with either an undo mechanism or a pre-release reviewer—never undo alone.

The Smartest Idea in the Repo: The Independent Goal Check

If you borrow only one architectural pattern from Stuart for your own AI systems, make it this one.

Most agent builders add a self-report at the end of a workflow: “Tell me if anything went wrong.” Stuart points out the two fatal flaws with that approach: a stopped agent reports nothing, and an agent cannot report work it never saw. Under quiet-by-default scheduling, a healthy quiet week and a completely broken pipeline produce the exact same output—silence.

Architectural Pattern · <agent-name>-goal-check

Never let an agent grade its own homework

For every ongoing, time-boxed, or repeats-per-case job—even when the human still clicks “send”—Stuart creates a second, standalone scheduled task named <agent-name>-goal-check. Its rules are strict:

  • Reads raw sources directly: It never names the working agent and never reads the working agent’s reports or memory.
  • Preserves deadlines across outages: For a 4-hour booking SLA, it checks whether any booking request older than 4 business hours is still unconfirmed—not merely “requests from the last day,” which misses breaches and false-alarms on brand-new messages.
  • Lives in a different home when possible: If the working agent runs locally on your laptop, the goal check runs in the cloud—because a check on the same computer sleeps when the agent does.
  • Knows when silence isn’t enough: Quiet when sources were read cleanly and nothing is overdue—except for rare, high-stakes obligations (like contract auto-renewals), where it sends a short periodic all-clear heartbeat listing the next upcoming deadlines.

Ten Opinionated Principles Behind Every Skill

Reading through all eight skill files reveals a remarkably coherent design philosophy. Every rule in Stuart’s documentation maps cleanly back to the specific skill that enforces it:

01 find-the-goal

A step is not a goal.

Push past actions (“send the email”) until you find an outcome someone would pay for or miss. The goal outranks the process, so steps that don’t serve it can be cut.

02 automate-anything

Use what already exists.

If an app you already pay for solves the job natively, use it instead of building an agent. Verify connectors and deadline math before designing anything.

03 capture-the-process

Judgment comes from real cases.

Three real examples—including one that went sideways—surface which source wins, what “done” means, and when to hand back far better than abstract checklists.

04 capture-the-process · design-an-agent

The saved cases are the test suite.

The agent replays saved cases before its first live run and before proposing any rule edit. Every mismatch is reported—never hidden—because your policy may have changed.

05 close-the-loop

The agent reads its own feedback.

Your edits to its drafts, customer reviews, and refunds become readable inputs. Approved lessons edit the agent file itself, while memory holds observations and cases only.

06 close-the-loop

Results land where you already look.

Drafts, outputs, and urgent alerts route to Gmail, Slack DMs, or your existing Notion/Sheets tracker—never stranded inside a tab you forget to check.

07 close-the-loop

Something outside the agent checks the goal.

A separate <name>-goal-check task verifies the real outcome directly from source systems, ideally from another machine/cloud home, catching silent crashes and blind spots.

08 design-an-agent

The final step stays with you first.

Agents start by drafting; humans send, post, or pay. Graduating to full execution requires a per-item check paired with undo or a pre-release reviewer—never undo alone.

09 design-an-agent · capture-the-process

Access enforces the limits.

Narrow tools and connectors to match anti-jobs—and stay honest that allowlists alone aren’t airtight when Bash, browsers, memory tools, or default-all cloud connectors are active.

10 close-the-loop · routine-healthcheck

Every run costs, so quiet is the default.

Nothing changed? End without a report. Schedules run as rarely as the goal allows, with deliberate exceptions only for QA checklists and rare, costly all-clear heartbeats.

Two Built-In Checkups That Keep Your Automation Fleet Healthy

Building agents is only half the story; over time, automations rot, schedules burn tokens polling empty inboxes every fifteen minutes, and new points of friction creep into your daily workflow. During setup (/stuart:getting-started), Stuart offers to install two local scheduled checkups. Both stay completely silent when there is nothing worth proposing, and neither builds or changes anything until you pick a suggestion.

Weekday Mornings · Local

Transcript Healthcheck

Mines your recent Claude Code session logs (~/.claude/projects/<project>/<session-id>.jsonl) using parallel subagents, skipping delegated subagents and scheduled prompts to focus purely on human-typed friction.

  • Hunts for frustration signals: “stop”, “wrong”, “i said”, “undo”, repeated instructions, redoing an agent’s output by hand, or abandoned threads.
  • Tracks its watermark in ~/.claude/stuart/state.json so it only scans new sessions since the last run.
  • Leads with at most 5 bullets and proposes evidence-backed skills, agents, or scheduled tasks.
Monday Mornings · Local

Routine Healthcheck

Audits your local and cloud scheduled tasks (cross-referencing ~/.claude/agents/ and repo agents) against seven concrete waste and reliability flags:

  • Too frequent: Denser than hourly (e.g., every 15 min = ~96 runs/day).
  • Noisy empty runs: Missing a stay-quiet rule on no-op runs.
  • Dead or duplicate: Paused for weeks, expired watches, or missing agents (explicitly sparing *-goal-check).
  • Missed/failed runs, wrong home, over-attached cloud connectors, and missing late-run sleep guards.

Local vs. Cloud Scheduling: Where an Agent Should Live

One subtle detail in agents/stuart.md shows how carefully the author thought through real Claude Code execution: Stuart explicitly handles delegated subagent mode. Because a subagent cannot pause mid-run to ask you follow-up questions, Stuart refuses to guess or build from an incomplete brief. If any required plan field is missing when delegated, it returns the exact questions to ask—one per line—and builds nothing.

What a Stuart-Built Agent File Looks Like

Every agent Stuart generates follows a tight, opinionated anatomy—YAML frontmatter for routing and least-privilege access, followed by an eight-part prompt body with zero fluff or “I can also help with…” sprawl:

~/.claude/agents/<name>.md · Generated agent blueprint Markdown + YAML 
---
name: booking-dispatcher               # Short kebab-case, matches filename
description: Confirm every inbound booking request within 4 business hours...
tools: [mcp__gmail__search, mcp__gmail__create_draft, mcp__calendar__list]
model: inherit                         # Or haiku (cheap/repetitive) / opus (hard)
memory: user                           # Observations & saved cases only (IDs/links for PII)
color: green
---

# 1. One job (first sentence)
Your one job: confirm every inbound booking request within 4 business hours.

# 2. Process (5–8 short steps)
# 3. Judgment calls (which source wins, what "done" means)
# 4. Stop & hand back (exact escalation triggers)
# 5. Feedback check (replay saved cases + failure on paper; report all mismatches)
# 6. Anti-jobs (adjacent work it never does, and where that work goes)
# 7. Voice (scrubbed full-text examples & banned words when writing publicly)
# 8. Quiet default (end without a report on scheduled no-op runs)

For coding agents, Stuart automatically bakes in three extra guardrails: short plain replies, reproducing bugs before attempting fixes, and adding isolation: worktree in frontmatter whenever the agent edits code alongside you so it never tramples your working tree.

Getting Started in About a Minute

Stuart is built to live in the Claude desktop app’s Code tab—not because you have to write code, but because that is where Claude stores agents and local scheduled tasks.

  1. Open the Claude desktop app, go to Customize → Plugins.
  2. Select Add → Add marketplace and enter bornio/stuart, then add Stuart.
  3. Open the Code tab and start a new session in one dedicated empty folder (like Documents/Claude—avoid Desktop or Downloads).
  4. Type /stuart:getting-started and send it.

Prefer the terminal with Claude Code CLI? One command installs the plugin:

Claude Code CLI install 
claude plugin install stuart --marketplace bornio/stuart

During onboarding, Stuart asks just four questions—one at a time: what to call you (and casual vs. plain tone), whether you write code (non-coders automatically default to ~/.claude/agents/ so repos never come up; coders choose repo-shared or global), whether you want the weekday/Monday checkups, and one job you want off your plate. From there, you’re already in Stage 1.

Zero Lock-In

Your agents outlive Stuart

Every agent Stuart creates is a standard Markdown file in Claude’s native agents folder, and every schedule is a native Claude scheduled task. If you ever disable or remove the Stuart plugin, every agent and workflow it built for you keeps working untouched.

Why Stuart Matters Beyond Its Own Plugin

It is easy to look at a 13-file Markdown repository and think, “Wait, that’s it?” That simplicity is the whole point. Stuart treats AI automation not as a prompt-tricks problem, but as a human-centered operations problem.

It assumes you don’t know your own process until you test it against three real cases. It assumes your agents will eventually crash silently unless an independent check watches the real world. It assumes you shouldn’t hand over the keys to send emails or move money on day one. And it assumes that the best automation assistant is quiet when nothing is wrong, honest when a website can’t be automated, and polite enough to ask one question at a time.

Even if you never install it, reading through Stuart’s eight skills is a compact masterclass in how to design AI agents that survive contact with real work.
